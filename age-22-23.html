22–23

Bronze row · ages 22 to 31

Five economic considerations

Graduate job

First full-time salary, taxed at higher marginal rates than part-time work.

SourceAustralian Taxation Office

Super builds

Employer super is paid on top of wages. The earliest years have the longest time to compound.

SourceAustralian Taxation Office

HELP repayments

Repayments are collected through the tax system once income is above the threshold.

SourceStudy Assist

Own rental

Rent takes a large share of take-home pay, especially in capital cities.

SourceMoneySmart (ASIC)

Travel

Working holidays and travel are common before larger commitments.

SourceSmartraveller

Forms and files to look for

  • Tax return (ATO)
  • Update details with employer (TFN declaration)
  • Rental agreement and bond forms (state)

Three income scenarios

Illustrative gross incomes: lower about $50,000, middle about $100,000, higher about $200,000 a year. For ages under 18, read these as household incomes. For retirement ages, read them as lower, middle and higher income and savings. They are examples, not official brackets.

~ $50,000 a year

Lower income

Entry-level pay plus rent leaves little spare. Sharing housing, living at home longer or using concessions can make the difference.

~ $100,000 a year

Middle income

Comfortable rent, regular saving and some travel. HELP repayments are noticeable but manageable.

~ $200,000 a year

Higher income

Strong graduate salary, rapid HELP repayment and early investing. Housing decisions can be about location rather than cost.
